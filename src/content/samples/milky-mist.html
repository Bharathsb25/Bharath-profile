Verdict

For: A genuinely differentiated business — 100% value-added dairy, 31.3% revenue CAGR, EBITDA margin up 180bps in two years, 19% share of organised packaged paneer and 35–40% of Greek yogurt, with 92% of the issue being fresh capital that cuts ₹497 cr of debt.

Against: You are paying 84.9x FY26 earnings — 105x excluding a one-off tax credit — for a company earning a 4% net margin at 3.6x debt-to-equity, against listed dairy peers at ~23x. The business may be worth owning; this price leaves no margin for error.

Cost bifurcation — where every ₹100 of revenue goes

Every ₹100 of revenue, FY25 basis (revenue ₹2,349 Cr) — the last segment is what survived.

Raw milk is 66% of revenue, and that is structural — it moves with procurement costs the company does not control, which is exactly what has pressured every listed dairy peer this year. The line worth staring at is the far right: after materials, people, distribution, depreciation and ₹86 Cr of interest, ₹4.05 of every ₹100 in sales survived as profit in FY26 (₹1.96 in FY25, shown here). Finance cost alone is roughly twice the entire FY25 profit — which is why the ₹497 Cr debt repayment is the single most consequential thing in the objects of the issue, and why RoCE at 11.73% is the honest measure of this business rather than the 32.1% RoE.

Amul and Mother Dairy revenues are group turnover, not comparable line-for-line. Cooperatives are unlisted and file differently. Milky Mist competes directly with all of these in paneer and cheese.

The competitive picture is harsher than the 19% suggests. Amul holds roughly 66% of Indian cheese and is the paneer leader nationally; Mother Dairy is second; cooperatives own 45–50% of the organised segment overall and, because farmer-members own them, no outside shareholder extracts a return from their procurement pricing — a permanent cost advantage Milky Mist cannot match. Milky Mist's real moat is regional and category-specific: dominant in South Indian paneer, strong in Greek yogurt, and only 5% nationally in cheese. The national expansion the valuation assumes means attacking Amul on its own ground.

The unorganised majority is the opportunity everyone in the sector is chasing; it is also why reported "market share" figures vary so widely depending on how the denominator is drawn.

Core strengths vs weaknesses

Core strengths

  • Pure-play value-added dairy — no low-margin pouch milk at all, which is why EBITDA margin (13.87%) beats Dodla (7.48%) and Parag (8.12%)
  • 19% of India's organised packaged paneer and ~60% of the South Indian paneer market; 35–40% of organised Greek yogurt
  • Integrated farm-to-retail: direct procurement from 74,654 farmers, 74.34% bought without middlemen, own cold chain of 63 tankers and 282 refrigerated trucks
  • Premium pricing power — products sell 10–25% above market average and the brand carries 97% of revenue
  • Growth is volume-led, not just price: volumes +20.5% a year vs realisation +8.6%
  • Anchor book carries Temasek, IFC, and A-grade domestic funds — 46.27% of the anchor allocation went to domestic mutual funds

Core weaknesses

  • Priced at 84.9x FY26 earnings (105x ex-tax-credit) against a listed dairy peer median near 23x
  • Debt/equity 3.61x and net debt/EBITDA 3.81x — finance cost alone consumed ₹86 Cr in FY25
  • RoCE only 11.73% despite a 32.1% RoE; the gap is borrowed money, not operating skill
  • Single plant at Perundurai, 94.51% of raw milk from Tamil Nadu, 69.23% of revenue from South India
  • Product concentration — paneer, cheese and curd are 59.05% of revenue
  • Inventory reported to lenders 7.7% below books; partial FY25 audit trails; ₹229 Cr unprovided contingent liabilities

Longer history — beyond the disclosed period

Bars are total income; the figure under each year is PAT. Only FY24–FY26 (navy) are restated audited figures from the RHP. FY20–FY23 (grey) come from RoC annual filings reported in the press and are not restated to the same basis, so they are indicative of trend only and should not be compared line-for-line. No ten-year series exists in the public domain — the company was a partnership until 2014 and only began filing as a public limited company later.

What the longer run reveals: profit went ₹32 Cr (FY22) → ₹28 Cr (FY23) → ₹19.4 Cr (FY24) → ₹46.1 Cr (FY25) → ₹127 Cr (FY26). PAT actually fell for two consecutive years before the surge. The headline "155.6% PAT CAGR" is measured from FY24 — the lowest profit year in at least five. Revenue growth is genuinely consistent across the whole period (~28–42% a year since FY20); the profit explosion is partly a recovery from a depressed base, not a step change in earning power. This does not appear in a three-year table, which is precisely why the three-year disclosure window flatters the story.

The paper-profit test: passed. Operating cash flow was positive in all three years and ran well ahead of reported profit — 7.2x PAT in FY24, 6.8x in FY25, 2.4x in FY26. This is the single most important check on an aggressively growing company, and Milky Mist clears it. The profit is real cash, not receivables dressed up as earnings. Note the FY26 dip in CFO despite profit tripling: more cash was tied up in inventory and trade receivables, so the CFO/PAT ratio compressed sharply. Worth watching, not yet alarming.
But free cash flow is negative in every year. Capex ran 1.6–2.1x operating cash flow annually, so the company has never funded its own expansion. Cumulative CFO of ~₹757 Cr over three years met roughly ₹1,211–1,590 Cr of capital spending, and the gap was filled with debt — borrowings rose ₹635 Cr over exactly that period, from ₹1,036.72 Cr to ₹1,671.85 Cr. This is the mechanism behind the leverage, and it is the honest answer to "why does a profitable, cash-generating company need to raise ₹1,428 Cr". The growth is real; it has simply never paid for itself.

This is the strongest argument for the IPO. Leverage falls from 3.61x to roughly 0.63x in one step — from the most indebted name in the peer set to better than Parag and close to Heritage. The ~₹40–45 Cr of annual interest saved is worth roughly ₹30–34 Cr after tax, or 23–27% of FY26 PAT, before the business grows at all. The valuation case rests on this: at 85x you are not paying for FY26 earnings, you are paying for what earnings look like once the interest burden lifts and capacity fills.

Value for money: Poor on earnings, defensible on operating profit. At 84.9x FY26 earnings the issue sits at roughly 3.7x the listed dairy peer median and about 49% above Hatsun, which has a longer record, lower leverage and better capital returns. EV/EBITDA of ~25x is the kinder lens — near Dodla's ~20x, though far above Parag's ~9x — because it looks past the interest burden that consumes much of the profit. On P/B, be careful which basis is quoted: 28.5x is on pre-issue book, but ₹1,428 cr of fresh equity lands straight on net worth, so the post-issue figure is nearer 5.7x — a premium to the peer median of ~3.4x rather than the 8x the headline implies. RoCE is the number that should worry you more: 11.73% in FY26 (9.54% FY25, 8.14% FY24) against a 32.1% RoE that exists only because debt sits at 3.6x equity. The growth is real and roughly 2–3x peer growth rates; the question is whether 3.7x the peer multiple is the right price for it, and the peer group being down 7–27% this year on milk costs is not a helpful backdrop.

This is the strongest part of the offer. At 92% fresh capital the money largely reaches the company rather than the founders — ₹496.8 cr retires borrowings (which should cut into the ~₹106 cr annual finance cost), ₹469.24 cr expands and modernises Perundurai, and ₹155.31 cr funds visi coolers, ice-cream freezers and chocolate coolers that support premium shelf presence. The ₹125 cr OFS by Sathishkumar T and Anitha S is small and follows a ₹125 cr secondary sale to Temasek in April, so the founders have taken modest chips off the table rather than cashing out. One caveat sits outside the objects list: a proposed Maharashtra project worth about 79% of the fresh issue, or 2.6x FY26 EBITDA, from December 2029 — the RHP does not present it as an unconditional liability, but funding it would likely require fresh debt, internal cash or more equity.

Promoter & lifeline

Sathishkumar T (CMD) and Anitha S — Sathishkumar T built Milky Mist from an Erode-based dairy operation in the late 1990s into a ₹3,145 cr value-added dairy business, deliberately avoiding commodity liquid milk in favour of paneer, cheese, curd and yogurt. K Rathnam serves as CEO. The company sources directly from 74,654 farmers across 25 districts. Both promoters sold ₹125 cr of stock to Temasek's Jongsong in April 2026 and sell a further ₹125 cr in this OFS, but retain 79.51% post-issue — a high level of continued skin in the game.

Why this matters more here than usual. The free float at listing is only 20.49%, so it is a thin market. The first real test is 13 Sep 2026, when half the anchor allocation unlocks — anchors took shares at ₹140, the top of the band, and those bought for a 30-day window rather than a multi-year hold have every reason to exit into any listing strength. The larger overhang is 14 Feb 2027, when roughly 45.8 cr promoter shares above the minimum contribution plus the non-promoter pre-IPO holdings become technically sellable. Promoters rarely sell at the first opportunity and Temasek is a long-horizon investor, so treat this as latent supply rather than expected supply — but if you are holding rather than flipping, these are the dates where sentiment and float can move against you independently of how the business performs.

Grey market & sentiment

GMP ₹19–20.50 → implied listing ~14% over the upper band, trend faded — the observed range runs ₹15 to ₹35, and the current reading sits in the lower third.

Anchor book: ₹465.29 cr raised on 10 Aug from 19 anchor investors, 3.32 cr shares at ₹140 (top of band). A reasonable book, though the QIB response in early day-1 bidding was muted while retail and NII drove demand — the reverse of the pattern you want to see. Overall subscription stood at 79% partway through day 1; category multiples shown here are indicative of the early pattern, not final figures.

Grey market premium is an unofficial, unregulated over-the-counter rate on a thin market with no settlement guarantee. It is easily moved on small issues. Treat it as a sentiment reading, never as a forecast of the listing price.

Summary
A genuinely good business at a genuinely demanding price. Growth is real and volume-led (31.3% CAGR, +20.5% volumes), margins beat every listed peer (13.87% vs 7–8%), and 92% of the issue is fresh capital retiring ₹497 Cr of debt — which is why Temasek, IFC and nine fund houses paid ₹140.

But you are paying 84.9x earnings — 105x without the one-off tax credit — for a company that keeps ₹4 of every ₹100 it sells, where RoCE is 11.7% against a 32.1% RoE. That gap is debt at 3.6x equity, not operating skill. The turnaround is priced as if it is already finished. Apply for the listing pop; think much harder before holding.

Who this is for
