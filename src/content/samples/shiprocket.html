Verdict in two lines

For: The core shipping business genuinely works — ₹187 cr of adjusted EBITDA in FY26, customer acquisition cost down from ₹4,101 to ₹2,829 in two years, and operating cash flow swung from −₹216 cr to +₹53 cr. At 3.5× FY26 revenue the headline price is below listed peer Unicommerce and 30% under the last private round.

Against: You are not buying the core on its own. The emerging segment burned ₹169 cr of the core's ₹187 cr, and on the revenue Shiprocket actually keeps after paying couriers (~26 paise of every rupee billed) the stock is at roughly 14× against Unicommerce's 4.7×. Add no identifiable promoter, 89% of revenue from 4.7% of merchants, and a pending criminal case naming the CEO, a co-founder, a director and the CFO.

Where the money comes from — and where it goes

Revenue mix, FY26

Core — domestic shipping & shipping apps₹1,485 cr · 73.4%
Emerging — cross-border, quick commerce, marketing, capital₹539 cr · 26.6%

What each segment earns

This is the single most important chart in the report. The core shipping business is a real, profitable, scaling operation. The emerging business consumes almost all of it. Net adjusted EBITDA for the whole company was ₹18 cr on ₹2,024 cr of revenue — a 0.9% margin that exists only because one half funds the other.

Emerging revenue is growing ~65% against the core's ~13%, which is the bull case. Employee cost alone exceeds 32% of emerging-segment revenue, which is the bear case.

Cost bifurcation — where every ₹100 of revenue goes

Merchant solution costs — what Shiprocket pays the couriers — were 69.39% of total expenses in FY26, roughly ₹74 of every ₹100 billed. That is the number that reframes the whole valuation: Shiprocket bills the full shipment and keeps about 26 paise of it. Everything else — employees, marketing, technology, depreciation and interest — has to come out of that 26 paise, and in FY26 it did not fit.

Where this is derived, not disclosed. The RHP's line-by-line expense bifurcation (employee / selling & distribution / depreciation / finance cost) was not retrievable from public summaries. The merchant-solution share is disclosed; the ₹32.6 "all other costs" block is the residual computed from total income minus PAT. Treat the split within that block as unknown rather than as reported.

Market share & competition

Claimed position

Third-party trackers put Shiprocket at roughly 33–40% of India's D2C shipping-enablement market. The RHP's own claim is narrower and carefully worded: "India's largest new-age end-to-end e-commerce enablement platform by revenue in FY25".

Read the share claim carefully

The industry report supplying Shiprocket's market-position claims was commissioned by Shiprocket from Redseer Strategy Consultants and is hosted on Shiprocket's own domain. That is standard practice and not improper, but it means the category boundary — "new-age end-to-end horizontal enablement platform" — was drawn by a party with an interest in the answer. Narrow the category enough and almost any company leads it.

The competitively meaningful fact is not the share number. It is that five couriers handled 84.5% of shipment volume in FY26 under no exclusive contract. Those couriers own the trucks, the hubs and the delivery staff. Shiprocket owns the merchant relationship and the software. If Delhivery or Xpressbees decides to build a merchant-facing dashboard of its own — and both already sell direct — the aggregation layer is the part of the chain with the least defence.

Core strengths vs weaknesses

Strengths — evidence-backed

  • The core business demonstrably works. Domestic shipping and shipping apps produced ₹187 cr of adjusted EBITDA in FY26 on ₹1,485 cr of revenue. This is not a pre-revenue story.
  • Real operating leverage. Core revenue grew 36.9% over two years while core staff costs rose 8.3% and other expenses 5.9%. An extra parcel needs courier capacity, not engineers.
  • Customer acquisition is getting cheaper. Core CAC fell from ₹4,101 (FY24) to ₹2,829 (FY26). 43% of 2.3 m monthly visitors arrive organically and 97% of merchants onboard without a human.
  • Cash flow has turned. Operating cash flow went from −₹216 cr in FY24 to +₹52.6 cr in FY26. The company now part-funds itself.
  • Near debt-free after the issue. Borrowings drop from ₹242 cr to roughly ₹32 cr on the ₹210 cr repayment earmark, against a post-issue net worth around ₹2,410 cr.
  • Priced below the last private mark. The ₹7,058 cr valuation is about 30% under the last funding round — unusual restraint for a 2026 startup listing, and it is why late-stage funds are selling at a loss.

Weaknesses — evidence-backed

  • Loss-making in every disclosed year, and the loss widened. PAT −₹79.25 cr in FY26 against −₹74.45 cr in FY25. The FY24 −₹595 cr included ₹244 cr of restructuring and ₹192 cr of ESOP, so FY25→FY26 is the clean comparison, and it went the wrong way.
  • The emerging segment eats the core. −₹169 cr of adjusted EBITDA against the core's +₹187 cr. You are paying for a profitable business and simultaneously funding an unproven one out of it.
  • Extreme revenue concentration. Power merchants are 4.7% of the base and ~89% of FY26 revenue — and that group grew by just 85 merchants last year, from 10,005 to 10,090.
  • Supplier concentration with no contracts. Five couriers moved 84.5% of volume, top 10 vendors were 55.24% of costs, and there are no exclusive arrangements. Tariff increases pass straight through.
  • No identifiable promoter. Post-issue promoter holding is nil. Largest holder Bertelsmann at 21.32%, then Tribe Capital 14.14%, Eternal 6.85%. No single party carries accountability for the business.
  • Pending criminal proceedings against key management. A 2022 case names co-founders Saahil Goel and Gautam Kapoor, director Arjun Sethi and CFO Kumar Tanmay, with allegations including cheating, criminal breach of trust, forgery and conspiracy. It remains unresolved.

Longer history — beyond the disclosed period

SEBI requires three restated years and Shiprocket disclosed exactly three, so FY22 and FY23 are not in the offer document. Public reporting establishes that the FY24 loss of ₹595 cr was inflated by ₹244 cr of one-time restructuring and integration costs tied to acquired businesses and ₹192 cr of ESOP expense. Strip those and the underlying FY24 loss was closer to ₹160 cr — which means the "losses cut by 88%" framing in the pre-IPO coverage is measured from an artificially deep base. Against FY25, FY26's loss did not improve; it widened by 6.4%. Restated FY22–FY23 figures could not be sourced and are marked not found.

Question one — does profit become cash? There is no profit to convert, so the conventional test does not apply. What the numbers do show is a business generating ₹52.6 cr of operating cash while reporting a ₹79 cr accounting loss. The gap is depreciation, share-based payment and working-capital timing, not aggressive revenue recognition. On the evidence available this is the reassuring half of the cash-flow picture.

Question two — does it fund its own growth? This one cannot be answered from public sources, because capex was not retrievable. It matters: the company is raising ₹885 cr of fresh capital of which ₹366 cr goes to marketing and technology. If ₹52.6 cr of annual operating cash were sufficient to fund the expansion, the raise would not need to be that size. Read the RHP's cash flow statement before treating the CFO turn as self-sufficiency.

Forward outlook & stated plans

The company states the ₹366 cr growth investment will be deployed across FY27–FY29, split ₹205.8 cr marketing and ₹159.8 cr technology, weighted toward the emerging business. Management has publicly framed the raise as an AI and platform-expansion push beyond core shipping. No forward EPS estimate from any named brokerage could be sourced, and at a loss-making base that absence is itself information: there is no published earnings number for a buyer to anchor on, so the price rests entirely on a revenue multiple and a belief about the emerging segment.

Valuation & value for money

Headline multiples — Shiprocket looks cheap

The same multiples on the revenue Shiprocket actually keeps

This pair of charts is the valuation argument in full. Shiprocket bills the merchant for the entire shipment and then pays the courier out of it, so gross revenue overstates the business it owns. After courier payouts roughly 26 paise of every rupee billed stays with the company — about ₹526 cr of retained revenue on ₹2,024 cr billed. On that basis the ₹7,058 cr market cap is around 13–14× retained revenue against Unicommerce's 4.7×. Unicommerce primarily sells software and keeps most of what it bills, which is why the headline comparison flatters Shiprocket and the retained comparison reverses it. Neither is a perfect like-for-like measure; both should be in front of you before you decide.

Value for money: Fair on the headline, expensive on the substance. Shiprocket has priced itself with visible restraint — 30% below the last private round, below the listed peer's revenue multiple, and a smaller raise than the ₹2,342 cr originally sought. At 54.7% fresh this is not a promoter cash-out dressed as a fundraise, and it is better than the majority of 2026 startup listings on that measure. Two qualifications. First, the identified objects total only ₹575.6 cr — ₹365.6 cr of growth spend plus ₹210 cr of debt repayment — leaving roughly 35% of the fresh proceeds for unidentified acquisitions and general corporate purposes. Money with no named destination is the weakest category of use, and here it is the second largest line. Second, the ₹365.6 cr of "growth investment" is marketing and technology opex, not capacity that shows up as an asset; it will run through the P&L across FY27–FY29 and depress reported profit while it does.

On the OFS side, the selling shareholders divide into two groups with opposite experiences. Founders and early backers are realising 77–359× their cost. Late-stage funds LR India Fund I (weighted average cost ₹133.94) and Moore Strategic Ventures (₹143.82) are selling below cost at ₹97 — roughly 0.72× and 0.67× respectively. When a fund accepts a loss to exit at IPO rather than hold through listing, that is a data point about how the last private valuation looked in hindsight, and it deserves to sit alongside the "30% discount to the last round" framing.

Founders & lifeline

Shiprocket has no identifiable promoter. That is a disclosed fact in the RHP, not an omission in this report, and it changes how this section should be read. There is no promoter holding to track, no minimum promoter contribution locked in for eighteen months, and no single family or individual whose net worth is tied to the share price after listing. Control sits with a board answering to a dispersed institutional register — Bertelsmann 21.32%, Tribe Capital 14.14%, Eternal 6.85%, KDT Venture Holdings 5.49%, Temasek-backed MacRitchie 5.29% pre-issue.

The operating founders are Saahil Goel (CEO), Gautam Kapoor and Vishesh Khurana. They have run this business in one form or another since 2012 and their weighted average acquisition cost is ₹0.27–0.28 a share, so the ₹142 cr they are collectively selling in the OFS represents 346–359× returns. Ages, education and prior ventures were not retrievable from public summaries and are marked not disclosed; the timeline below therefore starts at the first business milestone rather than at birth.

These are dates on which shares become sellable, not predictions that anyone will sell. But the arithmetic here is unusually lopsided and a holder should see it before deciding to hold. Free float at listing is only about 9.18 cr shares — 12.6% of the equity, because the anchor portion (44.97% of the issue) is locked from day one. A small float amplifies price moves in both directions.

Then the calendar tightens. Within 90 days the anchor allocation — the same institutional money being cited as validation — becomes fully sellable, roughly doubling the float. And because Shiprocket has no identifiable promoter, the entire pre-issue capital falls under the six-month lock rather than the eighteen-month minimum-promoter-contribution rule. On roughly 17 February 2027, about 56 cr shares — 77% of the equity — come free at once. Several holders of that stock are funds that have already signalled they want out: LR India Fund I and Moore Strategic Ventures are selling at a loss in this very issue. For a flip these dates are irrelevant; for a hold they are the single most underdiscussed feature of this IPO.

Grey market & sentiment

GMP ₹31 against a ₹97 upper band — an implied listing around ₹128, or +32%. The trend is the more useful reading: ₹14 on 7 Aug, ₹30 on 12 Aug, ₹31 today. Rising into the close of the book is a constructive sign.

Subscription, day 2 of 3

Retail at 3.33× is the strong category; the book was 0.97× at the end of day 1. QIB at 0.02× looks alarming and mostly is not — institutions habitually bid in the final hours of day 3, and 44.97% of the issue is already spoken for by the anchor book. It is still the number to check tomorrow afternoon before you commit: if the QIB category has not moved by mid-afternoon on 14 August, the institutional endorsement implied by the anchor book is thinner than it appears.

On the grey market premium. GMP is an unofficial, unregulated over-the-counter rate quoted on a thin market with no settlement guarantee and no regulator behind it. It is easily moved, particularly around a listing where the free float is only 12.6% of equity. Treat it as a reading of sentiment on the day it was taken, never as a forecast of the listing price. A 32% GMP has been wrong before and will be again.
